01

Beauty Advisor & Assurance

Zalando · 0→1 · Built all aspects including model and evals from scratch

What I built

An AI-powered skincare recommendation system designed around three increasingly proactive modes: Assurance, Advisor and Anticipator.

Assurance lives on the product page. It evaluates whether the product is a good match for the customer, returns a match or no-match verdict with an explainable rationale, and, when there is a mismatch, recommends better alternatives while using the current product as anchor. Advisor lives on the customer profile. It uses a guided diagnostic to understand skin type, concerns and preferences, then retrieves and ranks the five most relevant products from the wider skincare assortment across seven categories. The next evolution is Anticipator: moving from responding to explicit customer intent toward anticipating relevant skincare needs and surfacing recommendations at the right moment.

Why it was hard

Skincare turns recommendation quality into a trust problem. A recommendation cannot simply be relevant — it has to be suitable, explainable and grounded in evidence.

Suitability is multi-dimensional. The system has to reason across skin type, concerns, product attributes, ingredients and customer-review signals, while working with incomplete and inconsistent catalogue data. We built a parallel structured data layer to correct those gaps, then combined retrieval and ranking logic to distinguish between products that may all be individually suitable but differ in relevance for a specific customer. We also designed evals for an asymmetric cost of false positive.

Every recommendation rationale needs to be traceable to product and ingredient evidence rather than generated as marketing copy. In a skin-health category, a plausible but unsupported answer is worse than giving no recommendation at all.

Impact

~15% of skincare customers expected to adopt the feature
1.8→2.5x order frequency, the strategy this serves

04

ML-driven flash sales

Blibli · shipped · machine learning

My first shipped ML product, and the start of a personalization thread that runs through everything since.

What I built

A machine-learning rebuild of the flash sale engine: which products go on sale, how deep the discount runs, and when each slot schedules — across a catalogue of more than a million SKUs.

It replaced a manual, category-manager-driven process for 300M+ customers.

Why it was hard

A flash sale is a multi-objective optimisation — margin against sell-through against traffic — run inside tight time windows across a huge catalogue. Manual curation could not hold all three at once, and the merchandising team had to trust a model with the calls they used to make themselves.

The redesign, in shape

Five structural changes turned a manual, calendar-blind process into one a machine could run — and a human could still steer.

REDESIGN PILLARS5 structural changes
Surface One unified product feed, not a rigid category grid — with an optional filter, not a fixed structure
Ranking Top placement earned by discount depth, not reserved by category — merit over rotation
Ownership One accountable coordinator role with explicit CTR, conversion and GMV targets — not a shared, untracked responsibility
Budgeting A monthly calendar planned and shared ahead of time, tracked digitally — not discovered after the spend
Allocation Budget weighted toward what customers actually want on sale, not defaulted to the highest-ticket items

Impact

+5% overall company revenue
300M+ customers reached
1M+ SKUs priced and scheduled automatically

A

The Second Brain

Personal build · LLM-based second brain · daily use

What it is

I run most of my day through Claude Code, Gmail, Google Chat, meeting notes, Jira, and monthly business reviews. The problem was never capturing any single one of those. It was that they lived in six different places and forgot each other by the next morning.

So I built a second brain: every signal gets pulled into one Daily Note, cross-linked into a permanent Wiki, and made fully LLM-readable. Claude reads the whole graph — not a folder, not a search index — before it drafts a reply, writes a document, or opens a ticket. Nothing gets re-explained twice.

Why it earns its place

The diagram is a hub-and-spoke network, not a pipeline, on purpose. A pipeline implies information moves once, left to right, and is done. This system loops — outputs feed back into the next day's capture — which is the actual point of a second brain: it compounds.

Where it is going

Built with a small set of Claude Code skills chained together — capture, daily note, wiki, LLM context, drafts, decisions, tickets — running daily. It now answers my chat messages, drafts my emails, and writes management updates tailored to who's asking, among other things.

B

Hastings, built on OpenClaw

Personal build · self-hosted · runs every morning

What it does

Hastings is a personal agent that runs on my own machine, on OpenClaw, an open-source gateway that keeps the agent local and connects it to the chat app I already use. No hosted assistant, no data leaving the laptop except the calls it has to make.

It wakes at six. It reads my personal Gmail, my LinkedIn inbox, a standing fare watch on Berlin to Kochi, and about forty sources across AI, world, India and Germany news. By 06:01 there is one brief in Telegram.

What I care about is what happens after the brief. Every card carries buttons. Send the drafted reply to the broker in Kochi. Post the LinkedIn response. Arm the fare watch at €470. Write the AI items into my wiki as markdown, cross-linked to the essay draft they contradict. One tap each, from my phone, before I have left the kitchen.

About 470 items go in on a normal morning and 22 come out. The filtering is the product. Summarising is the easy half.

Why it was hard

Ranking, not retrieval. Pulling four hundred items and deduplicating them is a weekend problem. Deciding which twelve deserve six minutes of my morning needs a view of what I am working on this week. That is why the wiki feeds back into the ranker instead of only receiving from it. An item is promoted when it touches something already open in my second brain, and an open eval benchmark that contradicts a draft I am writing ranks above a funding round that does not.

Drafting in my register. The first version wrote replies I would never send. A longer prompt made it worse. What fixed it was giving the agent my sent mail and my own writing rules, then cutting the instructions to a tenth of their length. Durable preferences beat case handling, which is the same lesson my Claude Code configuration taught me.

Fares are a search problem, not a lookup. Cheapest to Kochi depends on date window, stopover, and how long I am willing to wait for a drop. Hastings keeps a price history per route and only interrupts me when the curve breaks its own trend, which is a very different thing from a fare alert email.

Trust, and the blast radius. An agent that can post on my behalf can also embarrass me. So the rules are hard-coded and visible in the interface: personal accounts only, work mail never connected, nothing sent without a tap, every action written to a ledger I can read. My Zalando context lives in a separate, air-gapped setup that Hastings has no route to. A capable agent with the wrong permissions is a worse outcome than a dumb one.

Where it is going

The news scanner is being widened past RSS into X and YouTube, where most AI news breaks first and where the ratio of noise to substance is worst. After that, a second agent for price comparison across retailers, which is a harder ranking problem than news and closer to my day job.

Listed here as it runs today, with the parts that are still manual left visible.

C

Vantage

Personal build · self-hosted · live

What it is

Vantage is a small web app I built and deployed myself. It's a chat coach for tech professionals working through a moment where how they come across matters — a promotion interview, a panel, a meeting with senior leadership in an unfamiliar business culture. You tell it what's happening, answer a few quick questions, and it gives you one concrete move for the conversation plus an outfit recommendation tied to your actual situation.

The interesting part is what happens after you close the tab. Sign in again next week and Vantage opens with your name and a reference to what you last talked through, instead of starting over.

Why it was hard

Getting the model to output clean, structured data. Vantage pulls facts out of a normal conversation — your role, your name — and writes them to a database. Asking the model for JSON and nothing else sounds simple, but it kept wrapping the JSON in a markdown code block anyway, out of habit. Every extraction silently failed for a while before I noticed, because I'd built it to fail quietly rather than break the conversation. The fix wasn't a better prompt. It was code that strips the wrapper before parsing.

Deciding what the model is allowed to be confident about. The outfit recommendations were the riskiest part. It would have been easy to have the model suggest a specific product or price, all invented, since there's no live catalogue behind this. The rule I landed on: real, well-known brands as a starting point, never a SKU, a price, or an availability claim. Getting a language model to be useful without quietly overstating what it knows took more work than the feature itself.

What I'd do next

Other use cases for generating executive presence — communication tips, networking topics, meeting tips. A real profile and memory system, recognising when someone's role has actually changed instead of just letting the old answer expire. And a real product catalogue, so the outfit advice can go from "try Uniqlo or COS" to something it can actually point you to.
